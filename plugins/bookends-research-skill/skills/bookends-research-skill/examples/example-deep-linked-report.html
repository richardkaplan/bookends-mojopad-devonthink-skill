Executive Summary

The claimant is a 59-year-old man who sustained an occupational hot-asphalt (petroleum bitumen) thermal burn in 2024 involving approximately 3.5% total body surface area of the abdomen and both arms, with disputed depth (partial-thickness per therapy records; full-thickness per a retained expert), superimposed on roughly a decade of occupational asphalt-fume, carbon-monoxide/hydrogen-sulfide and diesel-exhaust exposure. The driving question is how the risk of Marjolin ulcer (malignant/squamous-cell degeneration of the burn scar) plus any carcinogenic sequelae of the chemical exposure affect his projected life expectancy relative to the SSA cohort baseline.

Bottom line. Starting from an SSA cohort baseline of ~24.0 additional years (to age ~83.2), the literature supports a modest reduction. The central projected life expectancy is ~20.5 additional years (to age ~80), with a defensible range of ~18 to ~22 additional years (age ~77 to ~81). The dominant, best-quantified driver is a general burn-survivor excess-mortality signal (adjusted mortality rate ratios ~1.5–1.8), scaled down here for a small-TBSA burn with documented functional recovery. The Marjolin-ulcer risk is real but low-probability (~1–2% lifetime) with a long latency (~20–30 years) that would place any transformation near or beyond baseline life expectancy; its expected-value effect on years of life is small, though its tail (aggressive, metastatic, often fatal once nodal) is genuine. The occupational asphalt/PAH carcinogenic contribution is small and confounded.

Stance tally (18 sources): Supportive of an elevated risk/mortality signal 0, Equivocal 0, Not supportive 0. Most important caveat: the Marjolin lifetime-risk and latency data are limited and the burn-mortality cohorts are dominated by larger burns, so the point estimate carries real uncertainty; the Marjolin/cancer risk is most defensibly handled qualitatively (fear/risk of a shortened life) rather than as a large deterministic subtraction of years.

Life-Expectancy Projection (Method & Conclusion)

Step 1 — SSA cohort baseline (standing default). Source: SSA cohort life table, male, intermediate (Alt2) assumptions, vintage TR2024 (CohLifeTables_M_Alt2_TR2024.csv; retrieved 2026). For the male birth cohort, e(59)=24.16; applying uniform-distribution-of-deaths fractional-age adjustment at exact age 59.22 yields 23.995 additional years → total age at death ~83.2. Cohort basis is used deliberately (never period, never CDC/NCHS): it projects future mortality improvement across the remaining lifetime.

Step 2 — Literature-based adjustment (transparent layering). Three hazards are layered onto the baseline, each labeled as evidence-based vs reasoned judgment:

  • Burn-survivor excess mortality (evidence-based, best quantified). Adjusted mortality rate ratios cluster at ~1.5–1.8 (Kankam 1.59; Duke 1.8; McGovern HR 2.46 vs general population), but the anchor cohorts have mean TBSA ~15% — far larger than the claimant’s 3.5% — and much of the excess is early and driven by trauma/mental illness/socioeconomic factors. Because Duke found the signal persists even for minor burns (rate ratio 2.5), it is not dismissed; but for a 3.5% TBSA burn with a physical-therapy/FCE record of full range of motion and return to heavy work, the applicable multiplier is scaled to the low end (~1.15–1.20), corresponding to roughly a 2–3-year reduction. Paratz’s working-age-male cardiovascular signal (45–64 rate ratio 2.40) supports a real but modest long-term mechanism.
  • Marjolin ulcer (evidence-based probability, reasoned expected-value). Lifetime malignant transformation of burn scars is ~1–2% (Houlihan) with mean latency ~20–32 years (Alhadlaq 21.7; Burusapat 27.78; Bang 31.6; Esther 30). At age 59 with a 2024 burn, any transformation would typically manifest in his late 70s–80s — at or beyond baseline life expectancy. Even with high case-fatality once metastatic (Esther: all nodal-positive patients died; series mortality 12–37% at 2–3 years, Abdi), the expected-value life-expectancy effect of a ~1–2% event this late is small — on the order of ~0.5 year — with a genuine but low-probability fatal tail.
  • Occupational asphalt/PAH carcinogenic sequelae (weak, confounded). Lung-cancer SMRs among bitumen workers are modest (1.08–1.17; Boffetta) and the definitive analysis could not establish causation (Boffetta II); higher German estimates are confounded by alcohol/tobacco (Behrens); overall worker mortality is below expectation (healthy-worker effect). IARC classifies occupational exposures during road paving (bitumens) as Group 2B and benzo[a]pyrene as Group 1, giving biological plausibility (Binet). Reasoned contribution: ~0.5–1 year, highly uncertain.

Step 3 — Conclusion (central + range). Combining a low-end burn-survivor multiplier with small Marjolin and chemical allowances:

Projected life expectancy: ~20.5 additional years, to approximately age 80 (central estimate).
Defensible range: ~18 to ~22 additional years (age ~77 to ~81). The low end applies the mid burn-survivor rate ratio (~1.6) with partial realization of chemical/malignancy risk; the high end treats the small TBSA and documented functional recovery as placing him near baseline, with the Marjolin risk carried qualitatively only.

Candor on the evidence. Marjolin lifetime-risk and latency figures rest on case series and reviews, not population incidence studies; the burn-mortality cohorts are dominated by larger burns than the claimant’s; and the asphalt/PAH data are confounded and, in the definitive cohort, non-causal. The central estimate is therefore a reasoned synthesis, not a precise actuarial output. Consistent with life-care-planning practice, the Marjolin/second-cancer risk is best presented to a jury qualitatively (the risk and fear of a shortened life) rather than as a large fixed subtraction of years.

1. The Marjolin hazard: how often and how late

Burn scars are an established but low-frequency substrate for malignancy. Houlihan et al (2021) place the lifetime risk of malignant transformation at roughly 1–2% of burn scars, noting the lesion is “estimated to occur in 0.5% of vascular ulcers” and considerably more in burns. The defining feature is latency: the largest modern synthesis, Alhadlaq et al (2026), found the “mean latency period from burn injury to cancer diagnosis was 21.7 years”, and Burusapat et al (2021) reported that “The latency period was 27.78 years”, with Bang & Woo (2018) documenting a comparable “mean latency period was 31.6 years”. For a man burned at 57, a mean latency of two to three decades places any transformation in his late 70s to 80s — at or beyond his baseline life expectancy — which is central to why the expected-value effect on years of life is small even though the condition is serious.

2. Aggressiveness and lethality once it arises

Where Marjolin ulcer does arise, it behaves worse than ordinary cutaneous SCC. Shen et al (2015) describe an SCC that is aggressive with a “high rate of metastasis” (30% sentinel-node, 12% distant), and Abdi et al (2020) characterize it as a cancer with “notable potential for lymph node” and distant metastasis. The lethal tail is concrete: Esther et al (1999) reported that “All four individuals with nodal metastases eventually died of systemic disease”, and Shahla (2009) documented that “Wide resection is complicated with a recurrence rate of 20 to 50 percent and a metastasis rate of 54 percent” at high-risk sites. Cheng et al (2023) found that “tumor grade, tumor site, lymph node status, and tumor size are important predictors of a worse prognosis”, and that transformation is faster when the original injury occurs at older age — relevant to a patient injured at 57. The clinical implication is surveillance: a new non-healing lesion in the claimant’s abdominal or arm scars warrants prompt biopsy.

3. Burn survivorship and long-term mortality

Independent of any second cancer, burn survivors carry a measurable long-term excess mortality — the best-quantified driver here. Kankam et al (2022) meta-analysis (adjusted rate ratio 1.59) found that “burn survivors demonstrated greater mortality rates when compared to their non-injured counterparts”; Mason et al (2019) showed “Five-year mortality was significantly greater among burn survivors” (11% vs 4%), front-loaded in year 1; and McGovern et al (2024) confirmed an “increased risk of death when compared to the general population” (HR 2.46). Crucially, Duke et al (2015) found that “those with burn injury had a 1.8 times greater rate of mortality than those with no injury” — and that the signal persisted even for minor burns — so the claimant’s small 3.5% TBSA does not eliminate it. Paratz et al (2023) add a plausible mechanism: “Survivors from burn injury, especially young males, are at increased long-term risk of death from cardiovascular disease”. These cohorts are dominated by larger burns and by early trauma/mental-illness deaths, so the multiplier is applied at the low end for a functionally recovered small-burn patient.

4. The asphalt/PAH chemical pathway

The occupational chemical contribution is the weakest and most confounded strand. Boffetta et al (2003) found that “The SMR of lung cancer was higher among bitumen workers” (1.17), yet in the companion exposure analysis Boffetta et al (2003) concluded the data “do not allow us to conclude on the presence or absence of a causal link”. Behrens et al (2009) reported that “The follow-up demonstrated an excess of cancer in this cohort of asphalt workers” but attributed much of it to alcohol and tobacco, and Binet et al (2002) provide the mechanistic plausibility, noting “There is a possibility of long-term health effects following chronic exposure by inhalation or skin contamination” given the mutagenic, DNA-adduct-forming bitumen fume condensates. IARC classifies road-paving bitumen exposures as Group 2B and benzo[a]pyrene as Group 1. The net contribution to life expectancy is small and uncertain, and the healthy-worker effect (overall SMR 0.92) counsels restraint.

5. Integrating the hazards into a life-expectancy figure

Against an SSA cohort baseline of ~24.0 additional years (age ~83.2), the burn-survivor excess mortality — scaled to the low end for a small-TBSA, functionally recovered patient — is the principal deduction (~2–3 years). The Marjolin hazard, though serious, is a ~1–2% event arriving so late that its expected-value cost is ~0.5 year (with a real fatal tail), and the chemical pathway adds a small, uncertain ~0.5–1 year. The reasoned central projection is therefore ~20.5 additional years (to age ~80), within a defensible range of ~18 to ~22 additional years (age ~77 to ~81). Because the Marjolin/second-cancer probability is low and its data thin, it is most defensibly framed for the trier of fact as a qualitative risk and fear of a shortened life rather than as a large fixed subtraction of years — an approach consistent with how such risks are handled in life-care-planning and IME practice.

Academic Summary

Chronic burn scars are a recognized but low-frequency substrate for malignant (chiefly squamous-cell) transformation, the Marjolin ulcer, with a lifetime risk on the order of 1–2% of burn scars and a mean latency of roughly 20 to 32 years (Houlihan et al, 2021; Alhadlaq et al, 2026; Burusapat et al, 2021; Bang & Woo, 2018). When it develops, Marjolin SCC is more aggressive than ordinary cutaneous SCC, with substantial recurrence and metastatic potential and a grave prognosis once nodal disease is present (Shen et al, 2015; Abdi et al, 2020; Esther et al, 1999; Shahla, 2009), and its prognosis is governed by tumor grade, site, nodal status and size (Cheng et al, 2023). Independent of any second cancer, burn survivors carry a durable long-term excess mortality, with adjusted mortality rate ratios of approximately 1.5 to 1.8 that persist even for smaller burns (Kankam et al, 2022; Duke et al, 2015; Mason et al, 2019; McGovern et al, 2024), and a plausible cardiovascular mechanism in working-age males (Paratz et al, 2023). The occupational asphalt/bitumen and polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbon pathway shows only a modest and heavily confounded cancer signal, with the definitive cohort analysis unable to establish causation despite biological plausibility (Boffetta et al, 2003a; Boffetta et al, 2003b; Behrens et al, 2009; Binet et al, 2002). Applied to this 59-year-old man with a 3.5% TBSA hot-asphalt burn and documented functional recovery, these bodies of evidence support a modest reduction from an SSA cohort baseline of approximately 24 additional years (to age ~83): a central projected life expectancy of approximately 20.5 additional years (to age ~80), within a defensible range of 18 to 22 additional years (age ~77 to ~81). The burn-survivor mortality signal is the principal quantifiable driver; the Marjolin and chemical-carcinogenesis risks are real but low-probability and are most appropriately conveyed qualitatively rather than as large deterministic subtractions of life-years.
